How does Constitutional AI eliminate the need for human harm labels during safety training?
TraceLit 4 min ago

Constitutional AI replaces human harm labels entirely with a written set of principles — a "constitution" — that guides the model's own self-critique process¹. In the supervised phase, the model critiques and revises its own outputs according to these principles — bootstrapping safer behavior purely through self-improvement¹. The RL phase uses model-generated preference comparisons instead of human harm annotations — the resulting model reaches 96% harmlessness while matching RLHF helpfulness¹.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback and Constitutional Methods for AI Alignment: A Comparative Survey
Introduction

The alignment of large language models with human values has emerged as one of the central technical challenges in AI safety research¹. Three methodological approaches have accumulated significant empirical support: reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), constitutional AI (CAI), and instruction tuning with human-preference fine-tuning². Each makes different assumptions about the availability of human feedback, annotation cost, and the generality of the resulting aligned behaviour.

Methodology Survey

Stiennon et al. demonstrated that recursive reward modeling could substantially improve summarisation quality beyond supervised fine-tuning, using human preferences as the primary training signal². Crucially, their work established that reward models generalise to out-of-distribution tasks, suggesting they capture broadly useful quality notions rather than surface-level patterns. Building on this, Bai et al. introduced Constitutional AI as a method for generating preference data without any human annotation of harmful content¹. The model critiques its own outputs according to a written constitution and generates preference pairs autonomously — dramatically reducing the annotation burden for safety training.

Key Findings

Both RLHF and CAI achieve meaningful improvements over supervised baselines on human preference evaluations. CAI models demonstrate substantially reduced harmful outputs with minimal loss in helpfulness¹. InstructGPT's findings reinforce that instruction-following is itself a learnable proxy for human intent, and that even small volumes of high-quality preference data can meaningfully shift model behaviour³.

Research Gaps & Future Directions

A consistent limitation across all three bodies of work is the absence of cross-domain evaluation. Principles and reward models trained in one task distribution have not been systematically tested against novel domains. Scalability of human preference collection as model capability exceeds human evaluator ability remains an unresolved challenge with significant practical implications for frontier model alignment.

3. Constitutional AI
3.1 The Supervised Learning Stage (SL-CAI)
We begin with a helpful-only model and generate responses to harmful prompts. We then ask the model to critique its own responses according to the constitution, and revise them to be less harmful.
The only human oversight is provided through a list of rules or principles — a "constitution" — and so we call the method Constitutional AI. This approach allows us to control AI behavior more precisely, and with far less human effort.
We iterate this critique-revision loop several times, finding that multiple rounds substantially improve the quality of the revised outputs beyond a single round.
3.2 The RL Stage (RL-CAI)
The CAI training process begins with a supervised learning phase where the model critiques its own outputs according to constitutional principles. It then revises those outputs and learns from the improved versions, bootstrapping better behavior without any human annotation of specific harmful outputs.
In the RL phase, we generate pairs of responses to red-team prompts and ask the model to pick the less harmful option, using the constitution as guidance. This creates a preference dataset without any human harm labels.
4. Results
Constitutional AI models are rated by crowdworkers as both less harmful and comparably helpful to RLHF-trained baselines, suggesting that the constitution-guided self-critique is sufficient for achieving safe behavior without human harm annotation.
